# Writing the report

The reader is a pastor or church leader reading on a phone between meetings. They love their church, they suspect the website could be better, and they did not ask to be graded. Write like a designer they would want to hire: you noticed what they have built, you can show exactly what is costing them visitors, and you can see a clear way forward.

## The voice

- **Warm but NOT flattering.** Name real strengths with evidence, never filler praise.
- **Direct but NOT harsh.** "Visitors on a phone have to scroll to find your service times" beats "the homepage fails".
- **Confident but NOT salesy.** No "game-changing", "transform", "elevate", "world-class", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "unlock".
- **Visitor-first.** Describe every problem by what it does to a real person: the family in the car on Sunday, the widow with reading glasses, the dad checking kids' times.
- **Plain words.** "First screen", not "above the fold". "Buttons", not "CTAs". "Phone", not "mobile viewport".
- **Numbers carry the weight.** Lean on the score, the points and the measured evidence. Opinions without a check behind them stay out.

## story.json

```json
{
  "church": "Grace Lutheran Church",
  "town": "Decorah, Iowa",
  "preparedBy": "Your name",
  "headline": "...",
  "opening": "...",
  "strengths": ["...", "...", "..."],
  "topFixes": ["body-size", "tap-targets", "visit-page"],
  "notes": { "check-id": "..." },
  "closing": "..."
}
```

**headline** (one sentence, 12 words or fewer). The single most important finding, in visitor terms, with no score in it (the score sits beside it). Good: "Your Sunday times are up front; the rest is hard to read." Bad: "Your website has many opportunities for improvement."

**opening** (2–3 sentences). What the church does well, then what the score says is costing visitors, then the promise of the top three fixes. Mention the score's meaning, not the number.

**strengths** (3–5 items). Each a short sentence with its evidence: "Service times are in the first screen on phones and computers." Draw them from full-mark checks and from real assets (an active app, a livestream every Sunday, a strong building photo). Never invent one to balance the bad news.

**topFixes** (optional). Three check ids. Use this when the biggest gains overlap, so the three cards are three different changes. Keep them in points order unless one is clearly the foundation of the others.

**notes** (optional). Replaces a check's standard fix with one written for this church: "Your Wednesday Equip and Friday Jr. High details are great; give them their own Kids & Students page with check-in and safety." Use it on the top fixes and anywhere the generic fix sounds off.

**closing** (3–4 sentences). What a redesign would do in concrete terms (the projected score, what a visitor would see first), what the church would need to provide (photos, a few answers), and the next step: a short conversation about what they want, then three homepage options to react to. End on the church, not on yourself.

## The chat summary

After the report is written, tell the user in about six lines: the score and grade; the three top fixes with points; the projected score after a redesign; the report path and an offer to share the file. No full scorecard in chat.
